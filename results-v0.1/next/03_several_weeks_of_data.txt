RANK 3 OF 8: SEVERAL MORE WEEKS OF DATA
=====================================
Why third
---------
Calendar time cannot be compressed. Start collection as soon as correct
extraction, raw saving, basic recovery, and their small tests work. Continue the
remaining engineering and learning while data arrives. Do not wait for a perfect
dashboard, but do not extend known-bad quote extraction either.

Concepts from the beginning
---------------------------
An observation is one measured state of an item at a time. A sample is the
collection of observations you analyze. A longer sample covers more weekdays,
weekends, price conditions, and activity levels. A market regime is a period
with characteristic behavior, such as unusually high volatility.

More observations can improve precision, but dependence matters. The 12:01 price
often resembles the 12:00 price, so those rows do not carry as much independent
information as unrelated experiments. Seven items at the same time can also
respond to a shared event. More rows are useful; they do not guarantee a signal.

Step-by-step implementation
---------------------------
1. Read the validation guide before the collection starts. Write down the items,
   horizons, initial collection duration, and a future confirmation period before
   inspecting those future outcomes. This short plan prevents result-driven
   choices from being confused with an independent test.
2. Keep the initial seven items fixed for the primary study. Record the reason
   for that selection. Adding more items later is a separate exploratory choice,
   not extra independent support for a claim chosen from earlier results.
3. Choose four to six weeks as an initial practical target, subject to the
   power analysis. At one successful observation per minute, 28 days give
   28*24*60 = 40,320 observations per item before duplicates/gaps/exclusions.
   This is an ideal count, not a required or sufficient research sample size.
4. Run for about ten minutes. Open several archived responses, check receipt
   time and source time, verify quote sides, and inspect derived rows. Stop
   and repair obvious issues now rather than continuing a faulty pipeline.
5. Run a one-day pilot. Produce counts of expected polls, actual requests,
   unique source updates, valid rows per item, rejected rows, and largest gap.
   Check whether retries or cached responses inflate apparent coverage.
6. Choose an always-on collection machine. On Windows, Task Scheduler can run
   the environment's python.exe with the collector path as its argument and
   the repository as its working directory. Configure restart on failure and
   no overlapping instance. Test the scheduled task manually before trusting it.
7. Consider power, sleep, Wi-Fi interruptions, disk space, and reboot behavior.
   A laptop that sleeps overnight is not an uninterrupted collection service.
   If using a different host, document its timezone and environment as well.
8. Write all new receipt times in UTC. Keep source update timestamps too, but
   do not use source time as the time a prediction could have been issued:
   your program only knew the response after it arrived.
9. Review health daily. A simple CSV with date, expected_polls, successful_polls,
   unique_updates, valid_rows, missing_fraction, and longest_gap is enough.
   Keep per-item statistics; successful endpoint responses can omit one item.
10. Make backups of finalized raw files. Do analysis on a frozen copy or manifest
    of inputs rather than a file that is actively growing. Record the cutoff.
11. At the predeclared endpoint, freeze the research dataset. Do not stop early
    because a p-value looks attractive. If power planning suggests more time,
    make the extension decision prospectively and document it.

Small exercise
--------------
For a one-hour pilot you expected 60 requests, received 58, and got only 40
distinct source updates. HTTP success coverage is 58/60, about 96.7%. It does
not mean you have 58 independent market updates. If one item appears in only
50 of those responses, that item's completeness needs its own count.

What a good negative result means
---------------------------------
An adequately measured study may find no useful relationship under its design.
That does not mean collecting was wasted: it gives evidence about what these
observations can support. A short or highly dependent sample may instead give
wide uncertainty and leave the useful-effect question unresolved.

Current script connection
--------------------------
analysis2 audits whatever frozen CSV you supply; it cannot create historical
observations. Its input hash identifies the exact bytes read. If the collector
is appending, freeze a copy between completed writes before the final run.

Completion checkpoint
---------------------
You have a dated multiweek collection with known missingness, source freshness
information, backups, and a frozen manifest. You can explain actual usable
coverage rather than merely the date the process was launched.
