RANK 7 OF 8: A COST-AWARE OR SPREAD-AWARE BACKTEST
===============================================
Why seventh
-----------
Execution assumptions are meaningful only after quote semantics and prediction
timing are sound. A backtest answers a different question from correlation:
could a specified decision rule plausibly benefit after realistic costs?

Concepts from the beginning
---------------------------
A backtest is a historical simulation of decisions, trades, cash, and inventory.
Inventory is the number of item units you hold. Long means owning an item; flat
means holding none. Shorting means selling borrowed inventory, which you should
not assume this market supports. Start with long or flat.

The spread is ask minus bid. Buying immediately pays ask; selling immediately
receives bid. A fee is an explicit charge. Slippage is getting a worse execution
price than your reference quote, for example because available quantity is small.
Latency is the delay between observing information and executing a decision.
PnL means profit and loss. Gross PnL excludes some costs; net PnL includes them.

Step-by-step implementation
---------------------------
1. Read predictions from a frozen walk-forward run. Choose one item and one
   horizon first. Do not train a new rule using the same final returns you are
   about to call the backtest. Tune thresholds only on earlier development data.
2. Write the rule in plain English: when flat, enter only if predicted rise is
   larger than estimated round-trip costs plus a declared margin; when holding,
   wait the specified duration and exit. Negative predictions mean stay flat.
3. Specify initial cash and fixed small order size. Track a ledger with time,
   event, item, quantity, price, fees, cash_after, and inventory_after. Begin
   with one position at a time so capital cannot be accidentally reused.
4. Decide timing before seeing PnL. A signal using a response at t is known
   after receipt. Use a next valid observed ask after your chosen latency for
   a conservative entry convention; exit using a subsequent observed bid.
   A quote observed later is a simulation assumption, not proof of an actual fill.
5. Match the predicted target to the traded holding interval. A t-to-t+5-minute
   midpoint prediction is not exactly a prediction from delayed entry to five
   minutes after entry. Realign the training target or disclose this mismatch;
   don't silently pretend the two experiments are identical.
6. Calculate a hand example before writing a loop. Buy two units at 101 and
   sell at 102: gross price PnL is 2*(102-101)=2 coins. If artificial entry and
   exit fees are each 1%, cost is 202*1.01=204.02 and proceeds 204*.99=201.96;
   net PnL is -2.06. These rates are teaching examples, not actual Bazaar fees.
7. Verify applicable current fee rules and account assumptions before using a
   realistic rate. Until then, label scenario rates explicitly. Using ask for
   entry and bid for exit already includes the spread: don't subtract it again.
8. Enforce cash and size constraints. If you cannot afford the order, skip it
   with a reason. Cap size at a conservative fraction of displayed executable
   liquidity. For larger orders, estimate prices by consuming visible levels;
   liquidity beyond the API summaries is unknown.
9. Prevent overlapping positions and orders from spending the same cash. When
   expanding across items, share one portfolio cash account and use a stated
   tie-break for simultaneous signals. Check cash/inventory after every event.
10. Define missing exits. One policy is holding until the next observable valid
    quote and flagging the delayed exposure. At the end, disclose open inventory
    and its valuation convention separately. Never drop a trade just because
    its missing exit or poor outcome is inconvenient.
11. Test a flat-price round trip: it should lose spread plus fees. Test no
    signals: no trades. Test insufficient cash: no purchase. Test a missing
    exit and two simultaneous signals. Keep hand-known expected ledger values.
12. Run frozen rules across evaluation folds. Save trades.csv and equity_curve.csv.
    An equity curve shows cash plus marked inventory over time. Report trade
    count, net PnL, turnover, exposure, and maximum drawdown (largest decline
    from a previous equity peak), alongside cash/no-trade and a simple strategy.
13. Repeat prespecified scenarios with higher slippage, greater latency, and
    smaller size limits. Show results even when all scenarios lose. For uncertainty,
    resample time blocks of portfolio returns rather than pretending overlapping
    trades are independent. Few weeks seldom justify bold annualized claims.

Common mistake
--------------
Assuming passive limit orders always fill at a favorable price invents queue
information you did not observe. A first backtest should use clearly stated
immediate-execution assumptions. The midpoint is not a free execution price.

Current script connection
--------------------------
analysis2 provides chronological predictions, quotes, and fold identifiers as
inputs for this work. It does not claim to implement a backtest or prove profit.
Create a separate tested ledger module as the next implementation step.

Completion checkpoint
---------------------
You can explain and replay every simulated trade, reconcile the cash account,
and show how conclusions change with costs. An unprofitable result is retained.
